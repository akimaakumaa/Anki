BACK: [type: phrasal verb idiom]
Forms: blow /bləʊ/ — blew /bluː/ — blown /bləʊn/
**blew me away** = To impress someone greatly or surprise them in a very positive way. Used when something deeply exceeds your expectations.

Example 1: I was so nervous, but hearing them sing live completely blew me away.
Example 2: You've really blown us away with your dedication to this project over the last few months.

Watch out: Don't confuse it with "blow away" literally (like the wind). It needs a person as the object right after the verb (blew *me* away).
Similar phrase: knocked my socks off, took my breath away

BACK: [type: conversational idiom]
Forms: draw /drɔː/ — drew /druː/ — drawn /drɔːn/
**drew a blank** = To be unable to remember something or fail to get a response. Used when your mind suddenly goes completely empty in conversation.

Example 1: I studied for weeks, but during the exam I just completely drew a blank.
Example 2: We asked them for ideas on how to fix the bug, but everyone just drew a blank.

Watch out: Often paired with adverbs like "completely" or "totally". Ensure you say "a blank," not just "blank".
Similar phrase: my mind went blank, couldn't think of anything

FRONT:
1. I can't believe it's already Friday, this week has absolutely ___ <b>by</b>.
2. We've been talking for three hours? Wow, the time just ___ <b>by</b>.
3. Do you feel like this year has just ___ <b>by</b> faster than ever?

BACK: [type: phrasal verb]
Forms: fly /flaɪ/ — flew /fluː/ — flown /fləʊn/
**flown by** = When time passes very quickly. Used to express surprise at how late it is or how fast an event happened without you noticing.

Example 1: I'm having so much fun that the afternoon has just flown by.
Example 2: Can you believe our vacation is almost over? The days just flew by.

Watch out: It's typically used with subjects like "time," "the day," or "the week." Don't use it for physical objects passing you.
Similar phrase: went by in a flash, passed quickly

FRONT:

2. You didn't like the new office layout at first, but has it ___ <b>on</b> you yet?

BACK: [type: conversational idiom / phrasal verb]
Forms: grow /grəʊ/ — grew /gruː/ — grown /grəʊn/
**grew on me** = To gradually start liking something or someone you didn't like at first. Used when sharing how your opinion shifted positively over time.

Example 1: I wasn't a fan of black coffee, but it definitely grew on me during college.
Example 2: She can seem a bit harsh when you first meet her, but she’s really grown on me.

Watch out: Always followed by an object pronoun (me, you, us, him, her). It means the *thing* grew on *you*, not the other way around.
Similar phrase: warmed up to it, started to like it

BACK: [type: spoken chunk]
Forms: know /nəʊ/ — knew /njuː/ — known /nəʊn/
**should have known better** = To realize that a mistake was foolish because you had enough experience or common sense to avoid it. Used to express regret or mild self-criticism.

Example 1: I bought the cheapest shoes and they fell apart in a week; I should have known better.
Example 2: He should have known better than to interrupt the boss during a tense meeting.

Watch out: Frequently followed by "than to [verb]" when specifying the mistake. Pronunciation in fast speech often sounds like "shoulda known".
Similar phrase: should have realized, silly of me

BACK: [type: phrasal verb]
Forms: throw /θrəʊ/ — threw /θruː/ — thrown /θrəʊn/
**threw me off** = To distract, confuse, or cause someone to lose their focus or train of thought. Used when an unexpected event messes up your rhythm.

Example 1: I had my speech memorized, but the microphone dying really threw me off.
Example 2: Sorry, what were you saying? The dog barking completely threw me off.

Watch out: Often paired with "completely" or "totally." The object goes between the verb and particle (threw *me* off).
Similar phrase: distracted me, messed me up
