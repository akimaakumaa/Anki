FRONT:

1. I didn't mean to offend you, so please don't get ___ out of shape.
2. Why did you get so ___ out of shape about that tiny comment?
3. We shouldn't get ___ out of shape over something we can't control.

BACK: [type: spoken idiom]
Forms: bend /bɛnd/ — bent /bɛnt/ — bent /bɛnt/
**bent out of shape** = used when someone becomes unnecessarily angry, annoyed, or upset about a relatively minor issue.

Example 1: He got completely bent out of shape when I forgot to reply to his email.
Example 2: There is no reason to get bent out of shape over a simple misunderstanding.

Watch out: Remember to use the verbs "get" or "be" right before this phrase. Do not say "bend out of shape" when describing an active state of anger; use the past form "bent".
Similar phrase: worked up, annoyed

FRONT:

1. I know you want quick results, but a great career isn't ___ in a day.
2. Why are you stressing out when you know Rome wasn't ___ in a day?
3. We need to realize that a solid reputation isn't ___ in a day.

BACK: [type: spoken idiom]
Forms: build /bɪld/ — built /bɪlt/ — built /bɪlt/
**built in a day** = used to remind someone that important, high-quality, or large-scale achievements take significant time and patience to create.

Example 1: Don't expect your fluency to be perfect overnight; it wasn't built in a day.
Example 2: We should be patient with the new business strategy because success isn't built in a day.

Watch out: This chunk is almost exclusively used in a negative construction (e.g., "wasn't built in a day" or "isn't built in a day").
Similar phrase: takes time, a work in progress

FRONT:

1. I'm so glad that you ___ me a hand with unpacking those heavy boxes.
2. Who actually ___ you a hand when your car broke down on the highway?
3. We managed to finish the project on time because everyone ___ a hand.

BACK: [type: conversational phrase]
Forms: lend /lɛnd/ — lent /lɛnt/ — lent /lɛnt/
**lent a hand** = used when someone provided physical assistance, support, or help to another person to complete a task.

Example 1: My neighbor lent a hand when he saw me struggling to move the couch.
Example 2: We lent a hand at the animal shelter over the weekend.

Watch out: Do not confuse "lend" with "borrow". You lend a hand *to* someone; they do not borrow a hand from you.
Similar phrase: helped out, pitched in

FRONT:

1. I shouldn't have ignored his text because it probably ___ the wrong message.
2. Do you think your sudden departure ___ the wrong message to the team?
3. We didn't mean to show disrespect, but our actions definitely ___ the wrong message.

BACK: [type: conversational phrase]
Forms: send /sɛnd/ — sent /sɛnt/ — sent /sɛnt/
**sent the wrong message** = used when an action, behavior, or lack of response unintentionally gives people a negative or incorrect impression about your real intentions.

Example 1: Arriving late to the job interview sent the wrong message to the manager.
Example 2: I hope my sudden quietness hasn't sent the wrong message to you.

Watch out: Ensure you use the definite article "the", not "a" (always say "sent the wrong message").
Similar phrase: gave the wrong impression, miscommunicated

FRONT:

1. I absolutely love this new laptop, even though I ___ a fortune on it.
2. Did you really ___ a fortune on that fancy dinner last night?
3. We shouldn't have gone shopping because we already ___ a fortune this month.

BACK: [type: spoken idiom]
Forms: spend /spɛnd/ — spent /spɛnt/ — spent /spɛnt/
**spent a fortune** = used to emphasize that you paid a very large, expensive, or excessive amount of money to buy or fix something.

Example 1: I spent a fortune getting my old car repaired last week.
Example 2: They must have spent a fortune on their wedding decorations.

Watch out: Always use the preposition "on" when specifying the item or activity you bought (e.g., spent a fortune *on* a vacation).
Similar phrase: cost an arm and a leg, dropped a lot of cash
