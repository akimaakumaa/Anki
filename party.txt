FRONT:

1. You made ___! I was so worried your flight would be delayed.
2. I can't believe we actually made ___ before the rain started.
3. We barely made ___ to the restaurant before they closed the kitchen.

BACK: [type: spoken idiom / verb phrase]
**make it** = Used to express that someone successfully arrived at a place, attended an event, or achieved a goal despite obstacles.
Example 1: I'm so sorry, but I won't be able to make it to your dinner party on Friday.
Example 2: If we leave right now, we can still make it to the concert.
Watch out: Learners often say "You arrived!" instead of the much more natural "You made it!" when welcoming a guest.
Similar phrase: managed to come, got here

FRONT:

1. You can just leave your shoes by the door and make yourself at ___.
2. I want you to make yourselves at ___ while I go grab us some drinks.
3. We always try to make ourselves at ___ whenever we stay at this hotel.

BACK: [type: spoken idiom]
**make yourself at home** = Used to warmly welcome guests and tell them to relax, act naturally, and behave as if they were in their own house.
Example 1: Just sit on the couch and make yourself at home, I'll be right back.
Example 2: Please, make yourself at home and turn on the TV if you want.
Watch out: Missing the preposition "at" (saying "make yourself home" instead of "make yourself at home").
Similar phrase: make yourself comfortable, settle in

FRONT:

1. We have plenty of food in the kitchen, so just help ___.
2. I put extra towels in the guest bathroom, so please help ___ to them.
3. Did you help ___ to a drink while I was taking the phone call?

BACK: [type: verb phrase]
**help yourself (to something)** = Used to politely offer food, drinks, or items to a guest, giving them permission to take what they want without asking.
Example 1: Don't wait for me, go ahead and help yourself to the buffet!
Example 2: If you need a pen, just help yourself to one from my desk.
Watch out: Using "take yourself" or "serve yourself" instead of the natural collocation "help yourself". Also, forgetting the preposition "to" before the noun.
Similar phrase: grab whatever you like, be my guest

FRONT:

1. You can leave your jacket on the chair, and feel ___ to look around.
2. We want you to feel ___ to ask questions anytime during the presentation.
3. I tend to talk too fast, so please feel ___ to interrupt me.

BACK: [type: verb phrase]
**feel free to** = Used to give someone permission or encourage them to do something without hesitating or worrying about being impolite.
Example 1: If you need to use the restroom, feel free to use the one upstairs.
Example 2: Feel free to call me if you run into any issues with the software.
Watch out: Adding an unnecessary "to" before feel (e.g., "You have to feel free") or using an "-ing" form after "to" (e.g., "Feel free to calling me").
Similar phrase: go ahead and, don't hesitate to

FRONT:

1. I haven't seen you since our trip to Spain! How have you ___?
2. We missed you at the last team meeting, so tell us, how have you ___ lately?
3. How have you ___ holding up with all the recent changes at work?

BACK: [type: spoken chunk]
**How have you been?** = Used as a warm greeting when you haven't seen someone for a while, asking about their life and well-being since your last meeting.
Example 1: Hey Sarah, it's been ages! How have you been?
Example 2: How have you been since you started the new job?
Watch out: Using "How are you being?" (incorrect continuous form) or asking this to someone you just saw yesterday (it strongly implies a gap in time).
Similar phrase: How's everything going?, How have things been?

FRONT:

1. You must be exhausted from the drive. Can I ___ you anything?
2. What can I ___ you to drink? We have soda, water, and beer.
3. I am going to the kitchen, can I ___ you a glass of wine or some snacks?

BACK: [type: spoken chunk]
**Can I get you (anything/something)?** = Used as a polite, casual offer to serve a guest a drink or food, or to provide something they might need to be comfortable.
Example 1: Make yourself comfortable on the sofa. Can I get you a coffee?
Example 2: I'm heading to the fridge, can I get you anything?
Watch out: Learners often say "Can I give you" instead of the natural English phrasing "Can I get you" when offering refreshments.
Similar phrase: Would you like something, Can I offer you

FRONT:

1. I feel like we haven't talked in forever. What's ___ with you?
2. We know you just moved to a different city, so tell us, what's ___?
3. What's ___ in your department since you got the promotion?

BACK: [type: spoken chunk]
**What's new with you?** = Used in casual small talk to ask for an update on someone's life, projects, or general situation.
Example 1: Let's grab a coffee, I want to hear what's new with you.
Example 2: Hey man, long time no see! What's new?
Watch out: Replying with "I'm fine" (which answers "How are you?"). The natural response is either "Not much" or sharing a recent life event.
Similar phrase: What have you been up to?, What's going on?
